Machine Learning for Content Refresh Opportunity Scoring

A leakage-aware, client-held-out approach to ranking which pages a content team should review first — built and validated on a 30,000-row anonymized dataset, evaluated against a transparent rule-based baseline.

Dataset: 30,000 rows · 32 clients Validation: 80/20 client-held-out, seed 42 Selected model: Logistic Regression Primary metric: Precision@50
01

Abstract

Content teams often manage far more pages than they can manually review, making it necessary to decide which pages deserve attention first. This study asks whether historical visibility, freshness, content, and engagement signals can rank pages by their likelihood of being in an observed state of search-performance decline. The dataset is a 30,000-row anonymized internship release; the target, is_declining_label, is defined as trend_direction == "down" (54.2% of rows). A transparent rule-based baseline is compared against Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, and Random Forest classifiers, all evaluated on an identical client-held-out split so no client appears in both training and test data.

Logistic Regression produced the strongest held-out ranking performance on the primary decision metric — Precision@50 of 0.82 versus 0.26 for the baseline, with a ROC-AUC of 0.731. These are observed associations within this dataset and validation design, not evidence that the identified signals cause decline, or that acting on them will change search rankings. The practical implication is a repeatable, human-reviewed workflow for prioritizing which pages an editorial team examines first — not an automated content-change system, and not a model of how Google ranks pages.

02

Research question

RESEARCH QUESTION

Can historical visibility, freshness, content, and engagement signals be used to identify content that is more likely to be declining, so that teams can prioritize which pages should be reviewed or refreshed first?

A ranked review queue is more useful here than a binary prediction: it lets an analyst work down a prioritized list until their available review capacity — not the model's confidence threshold — is exhausted. The system is decision support. It ranks and explains; a person decides and acts.

Methodology

Feature contract

23 numeric features (search volume, competition, cpc, content length, 90-day traffic/engagement counts, visibility consistency, position, CTR, freshness) and 9 categorical features (competition level, content type, intent, and tiered age/freshness/length/impression/position groups) — 32 model inputs total. Numeric fields are median-imputed with missingness indicators and standardized; categorical fields are imputed with the training mode and one-hot encoded. All preprocessing is fit only on the training partition, inside one sklearn pipeline.

Baseline

visibility_percentile      = rank_pct(log1p(impressions_90d))
freshness_risk_percentile  = rank_pct(days_since_last_update)
stale_visible               = (days_since_last_update >= 180) & (impressions_90d >= 500)

baseline_score = 2.0 * stale_visible + 0.5 * visibility_percentile + 0.5 * freshness_risk_percentile

Computed only on the held-out test population. A learned model only earns its added complexity if it meaningfully beats this transparent, auditable rule.

Validation design

All models are compared on an identical 80/20 client-held-out split, built deterministically with random_state=42. Entire clients — not individual rows — are assigned to the test set, so no client's pages appear in both partitions.

Client-level separation is a stronger generalization test than a random row split: pages from the same client tend to share editorial style, templates, and domain-level SEO characteristics, so a row-level split risks rewarding a model for recognizing a client rather than learning transferable signal-decline relationships.

HONEST CAVEAT

Client-held-out validation is not time-based validation — it does not test performance on future data. It also produced a different label prevalence between train (55.5%) and test (39.1%), a real side effect of splitting by client rather than by stratified row. Passing this split is evidence of cross-client generalization within this dataset; it is not evidence of production readiness.

HONEST OBSERVATION

refresh_and_review — reserved for pages that are simultaneously high-probability, stale, and still visible — did not trigger for any page in this run. Fewer than 20 of 30,000 pages meet the underlying "stale + visible" condition at all, so most flagged pages are not simply old and forgotten. A staleness-only refresh playbook would miss most of what the model actually flags.

priority_review pages are the recommended starting point for human review — not automatic edits. A model score and reason code are a prioritization signal, offering a starting hypothesis for a reviewer, never an instruction to publish a change.

09

Limitations

  • Proxy target — the label is an observed trend outcome, not a validated measure of content quality or refresh need.
  • Association, not causation — reported relationships, including feature importance, describe what the model learned, not what would happen if a feature were changed.
  • Client-held-out ≠ time-based validation — generalization across clients within one window, not to future time periods.
  • Train/test base-rate shift — 55.5% vs. 39.1% declining rate, a side effect of client-level splitting.
  • Dataset scope — one anonymized release, 32 clients; not assumed to generalize automatically elsewhere.
  • Small held-out client count — only 6 clients form the entire test partition.
  • Single split evaluated — no repeated splits or cross-validated confidence intervals.
  • Threshold/queue-size dependence — metrics reported at one threshold and three queue sizes only.
  • Feature-importance limits — linear coefficients capture additive, not interaction, effects.
  • Potential distribution shift — search and content behavior can change after this data was collected.
10

Conclusion

Comparing a transparent rule-based baseline against three learned classifiers on an identical client-held-out test partition, Logistic Regression produced the strongest top-K ranking performance (Precision@50 = 0.82 vs. 0.26 for the baseline; ROC-AUC = 0.731), with content-length and traffic-consistency signals contributing most to the fitted model. The contribution here is a repeatable, leakage-aware, human-reviewed workflow for prioritizing which content pages deserve editorial attention first — not a claim about how Google ranks pages, and not a guarantee that any specific content action improves outcomes. Every result is an observed association under one validation design on one dataset release: it supports prioritization, not automation.
